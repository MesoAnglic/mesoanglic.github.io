Chapter Four: Settling the Household Finances, and Marriage

from the autobiography of Adachi Kenzō.

1. Sale of the Public Bonds

Among all the undertakings of my life, one of the greatest, as I think of it now, was the sale of the public bonds granted in commutation of our shizoku family stipend. My father treasured these bond certificates more than his most precious possession, keeping them stored away and never letting them pass into my hands. But the more I thought it over, the plainer it seemed that sustaining the family's livelihood on the meagre interest of these bonds was naturally impossible; better to sell them and convert them to cash, for even if the principal shrank somewhat, if that cash were put out at interest of one bu five shu or thereabouts, we could find in it a certain increase of income. I urged this upon my father from time to time, but as it came from a mere boy he would not readily trust or heed it. Yet my earnest entreaties at last moved his heart, and with the year-end of Meiji 15 pressing near, in December, he resolved to sell outright. The face value of the bonds was 655 yen, and since I surmise the bank bought them in at about seventy per cent of face, I recall the net proceeds came to a mere 486 yen 50 sen. I clutched that money firmly to my breast and walked home to Shimo-Chikami on foot, my mother following some ten paces behind me the whole way. She had taken the trouble to come along out of worry that I might drop the money en route.

That very evening we called on Mr. Tomoeda Iwao and entrusted the entire sum from the sale to him at one bu five shu, the interest to be received in monthly instalments; and from then on a certain margin of ease came into our life.

2. The Move to Shimashin Village

As I have related before, Tomoeda-sensei was a man of deep filial devotion, and after his mother grew infirm with age he closed the Tamana academy for a time and returned to his family home in Shimashin Village, where, while rendering her every filial care day and night at her knee, he carried on the education of his students as before. After four or five years his aged mother at last passed away, and it was decided he would return once more to the Kaseien in Tamana; his residence was then to be disposed of, and he said he would make it over to me at a very low price. The price, I believe, was a mere two hundred yen. Thus my family gratefully accepted this kindness and vacated our temporary lodging in Hiyoshi Village. The villagers of Hiyoshi, every one of them, saw us off with reluctant farewells, and there was such a commotion, each carrying some piece of our belongings over the road of a little under one ri to Shimashin, that our lively style of removal was reported even in the Kumamoto Shimbun. All this was owing solely to my mother's store of accumulated virtue. The new house had spacious grounds, and the soil of the vegetable garden in particular was rich, admirably suited to planting chrysanthemums and irises, so my father was very well satisfied.

3. A Family Council of Sorts

From about the time I reached twenty-seven or twenty-eight, my parents and relatives alike urged me repeatedly to marry, but I always turned a deaf ear and went on refusing. At last, on one occasion, the whole body of relatives assembled at my house and opened a family council of sorts: my parents had already entered old age, my mother in particular seemed prone to illness, so would I not marry soon and set their minds at ease and make life gentler for them. They tried to bring me round, but this time too I kicked back my seat and walked out. For when I weighed the various matters of my future, it was not merely that my will to marry would not fix itself; in the first place, on the household accounts as they then stood, married life was impossible.

4. No Room to Hire Even a Maidservant

Given the state of our finances, it goes without saying that even as my mother gradually aged and her body weakened, there was no margin to take in a single maidservant. By selling the bonds we had contrived some increase of income, and through Tomoeda-sensei's deep sympathy we had been able to purchase a house; we lived considerably better than before, and my parents were on the whole content, but we could not escape remaining much straitened.

5. The Old Sweet-Pedlar Woman Helps with the Cooking

About this time there was an old woman called Omasa who went from village to village peddling cheap sweets from a shoulder-load, and she somehow grew deeply familiar and close to us. Unable to look on at my mother's laboured movements, she would from time to time come in and lend a hand with the cooking. Sometimes she would settle in and help with her own trade quite set aside, and there were stretches when she came day after day for many days together.

6. Marriage

Proposals of marriage had been brought to me by various people, and each time I had refused, as related above; but when my friend Kikuchi Kageharu (formerly surnamed Asayama) urged me, saying that Ōsato Inokuma had a younger sister and how would it be if I married her, strangely enough I leapt at it myself. Truly, the marriage bond is a curious thing. Having merely glanced at a photograph Yukiko had taken with her classmates after the Shōkei school graduation ceremony, I committed to the marriage without so much as a formal meeting, signified my assent to Kikuchi's recommendation and asked him to proceed, and at once told my parents and obtained their permission.

As nominal go-between I engaged Naitō Gijūrō-sensei. Since Yukiko was a Shōkei graduate, I troubled Naitō-sensei, the school's principal, to take on the office. When I called on Naitō-sensei, whom I held in the greatest intimacy and respect, and announced that I would marry the Ōsato daughter and begged his good offices, the white-haired, boy-faced old teacher's whole countenance lit with joy as he said, "That will do very well"; his manner at that moment floats before my eyes even now.

The arrangements came together smoothly, but soon after, before even the day of the ceremony was fixed, I went up to Tokyo. Then the situation in Korea abruptly turned menacing, and in Tokyo I resolved to cross over; since I would in any case stop by my native place on the way, I reckoned to hold the ceremony then and set out afterward. The travel money for Korea had been conveniently procured and the wedding expenses could now be foreseen, so I thought at last to put my parents' minds at rest. Thus I returned to our house in Shimashin, and the moment I stepped through the entrance I announced to my mother, who had come out to meet me, "Let us hold the wedding the day after tomorrow." At the sheer suddenness of it she appeared thunderstruck. But at all events the Ōsato family had to be notified, and when I hastily sent word, they too seem to have been startled. The Ōsatos, expecting the ceremony to fall around the time the autumn winds rose, had just begun assembling the customary bridal trousseau, and were, it is said, genuinely at a loss.

Now when it came at last to holding the ceremony, my house was in a great stir over the preparations, engaging a family connected to us by marriage, the Yamamotos of Kawashiri, to undertake the dishes. The sake and food were thus provided for after a fashion, but the essential bridegroom, myself, did not yet own a crested kimono. Having only a single gauze haori to my name, I threw it over a striped katabira received from Sassa-sensei, and, possessing no hakama, went round to my friend Satō Keita's and borrowed a silk one. The outfit was managed after a fashion, but Satō Keita being short of stature and I tall, the borrowed hakama was far too short; I stooped as best I could with head inclined, slid the hakama down as far as it would go, and wore it in a truly odd, comical, and cramped condition.

The Ōsato house was in like case. The bridal outfit not being ready in time, they hurried out to Tōjinmachi and had crests painted onto a katabira as an emergency measure, and, the household articles being not yet prepared, she arrived bringing a single wicker trunk. That trunk I recognised: it was the very one Ōsato Inokuma had carried years before, when he and I went up to Tokyo together.

Thus Naitō-sensei brought Yukiko, and the ceremony was held at my house. Since the budget from the outset was to bring the wedding in under thirty yen, everything was simplicity itself. It was the thirtieth of June, Meiji 27.

After the marriage the question arose of how my first formal visit to the Ōsato house should be made. I had notified them it would come sometime before my crossing to Korea, the date as yet unknown; but on the evening of the day after the ceremony, around dusk, I took the mood of getting this too out of the way, and suddenly marched into the Ōsato house announcing "This is the first visit," whereupon they were once again startled and left speechless.

I set out for Korea on the fourth day after my marriage, and that was just on the eve of the outbreak of the war at Asan.
