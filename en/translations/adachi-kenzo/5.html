Chapter Five: The Founding of the Chōsen Jihō

from the autobiography of Adachi Kenzō.

1. Third Crossing to Korea

As related above, having accompanied the Asan campaign and seen at least the opening act of the war, I felt no further need to observe the fighting; instead I had come to hold a vague hope of starting some enterprise in Korea. So in late September of Meiji 27 I left Tokyo, stayed several days in my home village, and at last set out on my third inspection of Korea. That is, I departed home on October 2nd, on the 4th boarded the Osaka Shōsen steamer Shirakawa-maru at Bakan, and on the 5th landed at Pusan, where I immediately called on Consul-General Murota. Murota and I were on excellent terms, having hit it off splendidly in conversation the previous year when I crossed to Korea in the company of Sassa-sensei. Murota then said to me: "Would you not establish a newspaper company here in Pusan and publish a paper?" I answered, "I am no more than a mere student without capital to engage in newspaper publishing," to which he replied, "As for funds, I have my own calculations. I shall consult the men of means in this place and see to it. As luck would have it, a printing company here has just failed and they are at a loss what to do with it. It has a certain amount of type, and the machinery can be used as it stands, so I think you need only buy it up." I answered, "If you will do me that kindness, I shall try it," and decided to remain in the place for the time being.

Having as yet no experience in newspaper management, I felt the need to consider thoroughly whether or not to accept Murota's proposal, so I wired urgently to the Kyūshū Nichinichi Shimbun in Kumamoto, consulted Asayama, Uno, and Yamada, and urged Uno Shichirō, who had experience in accounting, to cross over to Korea. Uno came to Pusan at once and drew up a budget for the paper's publication; it turned out, unexpectedly, that the venture could stand on quite a small scale, and I promptly conveyed as much to Consul-General Murota.

Murota conferred with Sakaki Shigeo, president of the Pusan Chamber of Commerce (branch manager of the Yūsen line), and Miyamoto Higuma, chief secretary of the Chamber, and on the basis of my budget arranged a loan of four hundred yen, covering the purchase of the printing machinery and type together with founding expenses; and with that we at last set our hands to launching the paper.

In the interval we celebrated the Tenchōsetsu of November 3rd, attended the evening reception at the consulate, and so on; it was on November 5th that we took over the printing machinery and type from the Iwatate Printing Company, and the same day we moved it all to Benten-dōri. I then invited Ōhata Hideo and Kikuchi Kageharu from Kumamoto as editorial staff, further summoned pressmen and others from Kumamoto as well, and the paper duly went to press under the name Chōsen Jihō. No sooner had I issued its first number than I hurriedly packed my baggage and set out for Keijō. And this launching of the Chōsen Jihō became the foundation of knowledge I later drew upon when founding the Kanjō Shinpō company in Keijō.

I have never been one to keep a diary, but I did from time to time make records of critical junctures only. The founding of the Chōsen Jihō in Pusan was my first campaign in matters of enterprise, and a diary from it happens to survive; I also discovered the draft of a letter presented from Pusan to Kimura Tsuruo-sensei, but for reasons of space I omit it here.
