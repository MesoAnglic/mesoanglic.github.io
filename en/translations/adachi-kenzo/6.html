Chapter Six: The Kanjō Shinpō Company

from the autobiography of Adachi Kenzō.

1. Count Inoue Becomes Minister to Korea

While I was staying in Pusan for the founding of the Chōsen Jihō, a telegram suddenly arrived: Count Inoue Kaoru (later Marquis) had been appointed Minister to Korea. According to the press, the Count had offered at a cabinet meeting to resign the Interior portfolio and take charge of policy toward Korea himself as Minister. I had long known that Viscount Shinagawa was on intimate terms with the new Minister, so I dashed off a letter asking him to introduce me, and a letter of introduction addressed to Minister Inoue was promptly sent along. Consul-General Murota of Pusan, moreover, stood to Minister Inoue in what might be called the relation of a protégé, so he too drew up a letter of introduction for me.

On reaching Keijō I called on Minister Inoue at once and we conversed on various matters. I dwelt in particular on the need to develop Korea, and urged that for the enlightenment of the Koreans the first task, before anything else, was to publish a newspaper in the Korean ŏnmun script. Minister Inoue said he felt the same and agreed entirely; he introduced me to First Secretary Sugimura Fukashi (father of Sugimura Yōtarō), ordered him to lend assistance to the newspaper plan, and it was settled that I would draw up a concrete proposal at once.

2. Minister Inoue's Sympathy; First Secretary Sugimura; Kokubu Shōtarō

Before this, influential men among the residents of Keijō had drawn up a newspaper plan of their own, and their draft had already been submitted to Secretary Sugimura's office. Sugimura asked me as well to prepare and submit a budget for founding and maintaining a paper, so I calculated the start-up costs and monthly operating expenses on the basis of my experience and conceptions from launching the Chōsen Jihō in Pusan, seasoned with my own ideals, and submitted the budget accordingly.

Sugimura had served long in Keijō and was close with the residents, with not a few of whom he was of one mind. Since the application to publish had come from influential men among them, he had apparently made up his mind to yoke me together with them in a joint management, and he proposed as much to Minister Inoue. But who could have foreseen it: the Minister opposed the joint plan head-on. "That will absolutely not do. Do that and unity will surely be lost, and it will seed trouble down the road. Here, without qualification, let Adachi run it alone." At this one decisive word Sugimura had no choice but to give it up, and it was settled at last that the management would be mine alone.

Thus not only the monthly subsidy for the paper's operation but the founding costs and everything else were to be borne by the Legation, and the interpreter Kokubu Shōtarō (later chief secretary of the Korean Privy Council) stood between me and the Legation to handle all the arrangements.

3. The Contract with An Kyŏngsu

The newspaper enterprise in Keijō was steadily getting under way, but it was judged necessary at this juncture to ally with a suitable Korean man of influence as well, and on Sugimura's recommendation I joined hands with An Kyŏngsu. An was then serving as vice-minister of the Finance Department, and later became Minister of Finance; even among the high officials at the centre of Korean affairs he was a man famed for his sharp abilities. The substance of the contract with An came down to his providing the land and buildings in which the newspaper company would be housed; with the land and buildings he owned, that is, he made a contribution in kind to the enterprise.

4. Purchase of Presses and Type; Between Osaka and Tokyo; Kunitomo Shigeaki, Kobayakawa Hideo, Sasaki Tadashi, Ushijima Hideo, Yun Ton'gu; Kunitomo's Editorials in Chinese; Kobayakawa's "Account of the Opening of Korea"; the Paper's Credit

The question of publication being now ripe, on 10 December, with Meiji 27 drawing to its close, I set out for Japan to purchase presses and type. I went to a press dealer at Nakanoshima in Osaka and ordered two machines, a thirty-six-page press and a small press, ordering Japanese type at the same time. This was the day after my arrival in Osaka on the eighteenth, and I wired word of it at once to Kokubu Shōtarō.

The problem was ŏnmun type for the Korean pages: none was to be had anywhere in Osaka, but on inquiring around I confirmed that the Tsukiji Type Foundry in Tokyo held ŏnmun matrices. I went up to Tokyo at once, and almost before I had shed my travelling clothes at the Sasaki inn in Kanda Renjaku-chō I called at the Tsukiji foundry and completed arrangements to purchase eighty thousand pieces of ŏnmun type (two rin apiece in number-four size). On the afternoon of 27 December I left Tokyo on the homeward journey, boarded the Higo-maru at Kōbe on New Year's Eve, and off Suma and Akashi on the calm Inland Sea greeted the rising sun of New Year's Day, Meiji 28, celebrating a shipboard New Year that was, one might say, the first and last of my life, and a memory to keep.

Before this, the company's name had been settled in consultation with Sugimura, Kokubu, and the others as the Kanjō Shinpō-sha, after the proper name of Keijō, and it went without saying that I myself would be president; the composition of the staff, though, cost me some pains. The immediate need was a chief writer skilled in literary Chinese, and I judged there was no one for it but my fellow provincial Kunitomo Shigeaki; the moment I reached Tokyo I called on him, urged him to rouse himself, and at last obtained his ready consent. To support him the right man had to be found as editor-in-chief. Happily there was Kobayakawa Hideo, put forward by Hirayama Iwahiko, and I took him on. Kobayakawa had graduated from a normal school and was then serving at an elementary school, still within his term of obligatory service, so permission to resign and leave the province was not easily granted. I therefore approached Matsudaira Masanao, then governor of Kumamoto, negotiating through connections in every quarter, and by special dispensation he was permitted, term notwithstanding, to resign and cross to Korea. This was Kobayakawa's first step out of the world of education into society at large. For the editorial staff I brought in Sasaki Tadashi, eldest son of Sasaki Manabu of Pusan, and it was decided that Ushijima Hideo would handle the accounts.

Preparations now advanced without a hitch, and in buoyant spirits I spent New Year's Day and the second of January, Meiji 28, aboard ship, reaching Pusan on the third. There I joined Sasaki and Ushijima, who had been waiting by arrangement, and together we reached Inch'ŏn on the fifth. Leaving Ushijima alone at Inch'ŏn to receive the presses, type, and other freight that would follow, I went ahead to Keijō.

Ushijima loaded the machinery, type, and other equipment received at Inch'ŏn onto horse-drawn freight wagons and escorted them to Keijō; he reported that crossing the frozen Han River on the ice with wagons bearing that weight of freight was enough to chill the stoutest heart. The moment the machines arrived, the workmen who had come ahead and lay in wait fell upon them with a will, and working day and night had them fully installed in a mere five days or so. Only the foreman, a man versed in operating the machines, was hired in Osaka; the rest were all summoned from Kumamoto, and for the ŏnmun side we took on a Korean, one Yun Ton'gu, as the reporter in charge.

Now, the format of the paper as launched was four pages, the first and second in ŏnmun, the third and fourth in Japanese. Kunitomo Shigeaki's editorials in literary Chinese won great favour among the Koreans as well, and made the paper's prospects seem bright indeed. As one instance of Kunitomo's excellence in Chinese: with the Chinese compositor waiting at his side he would move his brush unhurriedly across the manuscript and hand it over without a single rereading, and there would not be one wrong character in it; his mastery of the pen was past all astonishment.

I held the conviction that a newspaper's credit lies in the promptness and accuracy of its news items, and the most important reporting I always did myself, calling daily on the Legation and other men in key positions and labouring at the gathering of important stories. Of setting out on my rounds against the cold early-morning wind I have an unforgettable memory. The Korean winter is bitterly cold; the ground freezes and turns treacherously slick, and once on the slope up to the Legation and once on the slope in the settlement, twice over, I went down hard, bruising my hip and skinning my knees, and for a while could not get to my feet.

As issue followed issue the Kanjō Shinpō won favour in Korea in and out of government; the confidence of Keijō came to centre on this paper, and single-copy sales on street corners grew quite brisk.

Of the complete change in policy toward Korea when Minister Inoue, having once returned home, crossed to Korea a second time, I will give the particulars in a later chapter; but around then Minister Inoue pressed upon me repeatedly the necessity of a palace strategy, saying he wished at this juncture to have articles written showing great sympathy for the Min family. An official organ has no way of refusing. On the basis of material supplied by Kokubu Shōtarō, Kobayakawa wielded a gorgeous brush in a series titled "An Account of the Opening of Korea," discussing Korean state affairs under the thesis that the Taewŏn'gun's faction had always been the party of seclusion while the Min faction had pursued a policy of opening and advancement, and day after day the paper carried his flowing prose. The articles were, it seems, indeed welcomed at the palace, and by the Minister's particular instruction three hundred copies a day, done up in a single bundle, were carried into the palace. This was the chief outward form Minister Inoue's palace policy took.

5. Visiting My Sick Father; Yukiko Crosses to Korea

On 9 June of the same year, receiving word that my father's illness was grave, I hurried home to Kumamoto. His illness was a gastric ulcer, but it proved unexpectedly light and he soon recovered, so this time I resolved to bring Yukiko with me to Korea.

On the crossing I was to travel aboard the government vessel carrying Minister Inoue on his second mission, and I was at a loss what to do about Yukiko; but as luck would have it, just then Satō Junzō, proceeding to his post as assistant adviser to the Korean Finance Department, happened to be crossing with his family, so I asked that Yukiko be added to his party, and arranged to receive her at Inch'ŏn.
